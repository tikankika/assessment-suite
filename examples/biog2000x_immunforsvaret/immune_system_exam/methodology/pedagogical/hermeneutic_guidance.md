## Purpose

This document is loaded by the hermeneutic circle tool and returned alongside Phase 6 assessment data when Claude requests a re-reading during Phase 9–14.

It contains **contextual questions** — not answers, not instructions, but prompts for reflection. These are the questions an experienced assessor would ask themselves at each step. Claude does not have professional judgment; this document provides a structured substitute grounded in assessment theory.

**This document does NOT replace phase methodology documents.** Phase methodology documents describe WHAT to do (steps, output format, quality criteria). This document describes HOW TO THINK while doing it.

### How the tool uses this document

When Claude calls the tool with `phase`, `step`, and `question_ids`:

1. The tool extracts the relevant Phase 6 assessment text (the data)
2. The tool loads the relevant section from THIS document (the guidance)
3. Both are returned together

Claude reads the data through the lens of the questions. The teacher confirms or challenges the interpretation.

## Phase 9: Hermeneutisk syntes

**Theoretical frame:** Moss (1994, 2003) — the hermeneutic circle. Form an understanding of the whole, examine the parts, revise the whole.

### STEG 1: Områdesvis analys

*You are grouping questions by subject area and writing area-level observations.*

When re-reading specific question assessments, ask yourself:

- **Koherens:** Does this question's assessment fit the area pattern you're building? If not — is the question in the wrong area, or is the area pattern wrong?
- **Observation vs tolkning:** Are you describing what the student *wrote*, or what you think the student *meant*? Stay with the observation first.
- **Kvalitativ information:** The score is a number. What does the *text* of the assessment tell you that the number doesn't? A 2/4 with "partially correct reasoning but wrong conclusion" is different from a 2/4 with "correct conclusion but no reasoning."

### STEG 2: Övergripande mönster

*You are looking for patterns that cut across areas. This is where the hermeneutic circle is most active.*

When re-reading specific question assessments, ask yourself:

- **Helhet → del:** You have an emerging picture of this student. Does what you're reading now *confirm* that picture, or does it *challenge* it? If it challenges — revise the picture, don't ignore the evidence.
- **Del → helhet:** Now that you've re-read this specific assessment — does the overall pattern look different? Has something shifted?
- **Mönster eller artefakt:** Is this a pattern in the student's *understanding*, or an artifact of the *question design*? (Moss 2003: attend to context.) If three students show the same "weakness," the question may be the problem.
- **Kvalitativ likhet:** You're looking for qualitative similarity, not identical scores. Two students who both score 1/4 may have completely different understandings. Read the text, not just the number.
- **Spänning som signal:** When quantitative scores and qualitative observations disagree — that tension is informative. Don't resolve it by picking one; document both.

### STEG 3: Övergripande generalisering

*You are producing the final knowledge profile. This is the hermeneutic "revised whole."*

When re-reading specific question assessments, ask yourself:

- **Är profilen en syntes eller en lista?** A knowledge profile that lists "strong in X, weak in Y, moderate in Z" is not a synthesis. A synthesis says something about the *relationship* between X, Y, and Z.
- **Moss-testet:** Could another assessor, reading the same evidence, arrive at a substantially different but equally defensible interpretation? If yes — your profile should acknowledge the ambiguity, not hide it.
- **Scope:** Does your profile claim more than the exam evidence supports? "This student does not understand ecology" is a claim about the student. "This exam does not demonstrate ecology understanding" is a claim about the evidence. Use the second form.

## Phase 10: Extrapolering

**Theoretical frame:** Kane (2006) — the extrapolation inference. Can we move from what the exam shows to what the student can do in the broader course domain?

### STEG 1: Kriteriematchning

*You are mapping exam evidence to course criteria.*

When re-reading specific question assessments, ask yourself:

- **Evidens eller antagande?** Does this question actually *assess* this criterion, or are you assuming it does because the topic is related? One question about ecosystems does not constitute full evidence for an ILO about ecological systems thinking.
- **Confidence calibration:** If you marked confidence as "high" — is it genuinely high, or is it "high because the student scored well"? High confidence means the exam *format and coverage* support the inference, not just the score.
- **Representativitet:** Kane (2006): extrapolation is only as strong as the representativeness of the test tasks. If the exam only tested factual recall, you cannot extrapolate to analytical ability — regardless of the score.

### STEG 2: Kritiska tolkningar

*You are making interpretive decisions about compensation, weighting, and generalizability.*

When re-reading specific question assessments, ask yourself:

- **Is this a student-level decision or a course-level decision?** Compensation and weighting rules should be consistent across students. If you're making a different compensation decision for this student — why? (Known issue: STEG 2 was identical for all students in KK3.)
- **Generaliserbarhetens gränser:** What would this student need to demonstrate *outside this exam* to strengthen or weaken the extrapolation? Be specific.

### STEG 3: Sammanfattning

- **Does the summary follow from STEG 1-2, or does it introduce new claims?** If something appears in STEG 3 that isn't warranted by STEG 1-2, it shouldn't be there.

## Phase 11: Betygsbeslut

**Theoretical frame:** Kane (2006) interpretation/use inference, Messick (1989) consequential validity, Sadler (1989) holistic quality perception.

### STEG 1: Evidenssammanfattning

*You are compiling Phase 10 criterion indications for the teacher.*

- **Present, don't propose.** AI does not propose a grade. Present the evidence and ask the teacher.
- **Highlight uncertainty.** Low-confidence indications should be visually prominent, not buried.

### STEG 2: Tolkningsbeslut

*The teacher is making interpretive decisions.*

When re-reading specific question assessments (especially for borderline cases), ask yourself:

- **Sadler: Helhetsbilden.** Ignore the numbers for a moment. What quality level does this student's work *as a whole* convey? Does the holistic impression match the analytic breakdown? If not — which do you trust?
- **Messick: Konsekvenser.** What happens to this student if the grade is one step higher? One step lower? Do the consequences match the evidence strength?
- **Kane: Inferenskedjan.** Can you trace the path from specific answers → Phase 9 synthesis → Phase 10 criterion indications → this grade? Is every link warranted?
- **Gränsfall:** If this is a borderline case — read the student's actual answers again, not just the summaries. The summaries may have lost nuance that matters at the boundary.

### STEG 3: Betygsbeslut

- **The teacher states the grade.** Document the reasoning. Another teacher should be able to read the rationale and understand why this grade, not an adjacent one.

## Phase 12: Formativ återkoppling

**Theoretical frame:** Hattie & Timperley (2007) three feedback questions, Sadler (1989) quality gap perception.

### STEG 1: Var är eleven nu?

*You are summarizing the student's performance.*

When re-reading specific question assessments, ask yourself:

- **Styrka eller frånvaro av svaghet?** Is this a genuine strength (the student did something well), or is it just "didn't fail here"? Genuine strengths are things the student can build on.
- **Sadler: Kan eleven se gapet?** If you describe the student's current position — would the student recognize themselves? Or would they be surprised? Feedback that doesn't match the student's self-perception may be rejected.

### STEG 3: Hur tar eleven sig dit?

*You are writing strategies.*

When re-reading specific question assessments, ask yourself:

- **Bygger strategin på styrkor?** A strategy that says "do more of X" when the student has never shown X is useless. A strategy that says "you did X well on Q3 — apply the same approach to Q7-type questions" builds on demonstrated capability.
- **Parasuraman: Kommer eleven att läsa detta?** If the feedback is longer than one page, the student probably won't read it. Prioritize. What is the ONE thing this student should do differently?

## Phase 13: Klassanalys

**Theoretical frame:** Black & Wiliam (2009) — assessment evidence for instructional decisions.

### DEL 2: Kollektiva mönster

*You are looking for patterns across students.*

When re-reading specific question assessments (across multiple students), ask yourself:

- **Mönster eller fråge-artefakt?** If 10/14 students show the same "weakness" on Q6, is Q6 poorly designed, or is there a genuine class-level gap? Read the actual student answers — do they fail in the same way?
- **Kvalitativ likhet (Moss):** You're grouping students who show "similar" difficulties. Similar means the same *type* of difficulty, not the same *score*. Read the Phase 9 profiles, not just the numbers.
- **Black & Wiliam: Kan läraren agera?** If you identify a pattern but the teacher can't do anything about it — is it useful to report? Phase 13 should focus on actionable patterns.

### DEL 3: Stödstrukturer

- **No thresholds.** Do not group students by percentage. Group by qualitative need — what kind of support does this student need? Read Phase 9 profiles to determine this, not Phase 8 scores.

## Phase 14: Elevåterkoppling

**Theoretical frame:** Hattie & Timperley (2007), Sadler (1989). Audience: the student.

When re-reading specific question assessments, ask yourself:

- **Would the student understand this?** Phase 12 language is for the teacher. Phase 14 language is for the student. If you're using assessment terminology, rewrite.
- **Parasuraman: Längd.** ~300 words full, ~150 words short (narrative only, excluding per-question table). If you're over — cut. Unread feedback is no feedback.
- **Hattie & Timperley: Feed-forward.** Is there at least one concrete "do this next" in the feedback? If the student reads this and thinks "okay, but what should I actually do?" — the feedback has failed.

## Design Notes

### Why questions, not instructions

Instructions tell Claude what to do. Questions tell Claude what to *consider*. The difference matters:

- Instruction: "Check whether the pattern is coherent." → Claude writes "The pattern is coherent." (Compliance without thought.)
- Question: "Does the pattern cohere with your emerging interpretation? Or does it challenge it?" → Claude must actually evaluate, and the answer might be "it challenges it."

Questions create genuine reflection. Instructions create compliance.

### Why loaded from a document, not hardcoded

These questions will change as the theoretical work progresses. Moss (1994) primary reading may reveal that the hermeneutic circle operates differently than described here. Messick (1989) may suggest different consequential validity questions. By keeping the questions in a methodology document loaded by `MethodologyLoader`, we can revise them without changing the tool code.
